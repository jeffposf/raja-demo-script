Act 2 — Behind the scenes: how a business user builds it (30 min)

Switch to Salesforce, Personalization app.

Screen 2.1 — The three building blocks (slide or whiteboard)

Show: nothing on screen yet, or a simple drawing: a salesperson, a shelf, an instruction card.

Say: "Everything you saw is built with three building blocks. If you remember three words from today, it's these. Think of a shop. One: the recommender. That's the salesperson who picks products. Each salesperson has a method: one picks 'what people buy together', another picks 'similar products', another one is an AI that learns from each customer. A recommender answers one question: which products? Two: the personalization point. That's a spot in the shop window: the hero banner, the 'customers also bought' zone, the cart zone. A personalization point answers: where? Three: the personalization decision. That's the instruction card stuck on the shelf: 'for frequent buyers, use the AI salesperson; for everyone else, use the interests salesperson'. A decision answers: for whom, and with what? Recommender, point, decision. Which products, where, for whom. Let me spend one more minute on each block. A recommender comes in two kinds. Rule-based: you choose the ranking, from a calculation in Data 360: top sellers, most viewed, bought together. Transparent, works from day one, and you can explain every result. AI, or objective-based: you choose a goal, add to cart, clicks or revenue, and a deep-learning model ranks products for each person. It retrains every day, and it can recommend a brand-new product from its description. A personalization point is a zone on the site, plus its format: which product fields come back, and how many products. A decision is a rule on that zone: who it's for, with which recommender or content, and in which priority. One visitor, one decision per zone."

UC: UC1.

Screen 2.2 — Recommenders

Show: Recommenders list (8 recommenders). Open RAJA Co-Buy, then RAJA ML add to cart V2.

Say: "Here are our salespeople. Two kinds. Rule-based ones, like Co-Buy: we tell it 'rank products by how often they're bought together with the product on screen'. It's transparent: you can explain every result. And AI ones, like 'ML add to cart': we give it a goal, here 'maximise add-to-cart', and a deep-learning model finds the products most likely to reach that goal for this person. It can also recommend new products from their description, before anyone has bought them. On top of the AI, the marketer keeps control with filters. Inclusion: 'only recommend products that…'. Exclusion: 'never recommend products that…'. A filter can compare a product with a fixed value, with the product on screen, or with anything we know about the customer in Data 360. Inclusion examples: only products in stock. Only products of the same colour or range as the one on screen. Only products under a price, for a budget zone. Only products of a seasonal range between two dates. Exclusion examples: here, the product the customer is looking at right now, otherwise the AI could suggest the very box he already has on screen. Products he has already bought, though for RAJA consumables you may want the opposite. Expired offers. Clearance items in a full-price zone. And customer data works on both levels. A segment like 'frequent buyers' can choose who gets this recommender, in the decision rule, as you'll see in a minute. It can also choose which products: for example, products reserved for members of a segment. Each recommender can have a backup: if it can't find enough products, it falls back to top sellers. The zone is never empty."

UC: UC1, UC3.

Act 4 — Testing and measuring (15 min)

Screen 4.1 — The experiment

Show: Experiments tab → "PDP Also Bought Co-Buy vs ML" (on personalization point "PDP Customers Also Bought"): two variations 50/50, "Business as Usual" (control, "Defer response to the Personalization Decisions") and "ML add to cart"; primary metric RAJA Add To Cart, secondary Purchase and Product View.

Say: "How do you know the AI does better than a simple rule? You test it, directly on the zone. Four settings. One, the primary metric: the one that decides the winner, here add to cart. Secondary metrics, purchases and product views, are watched but don't decide. Two, who enters the test: everyone, or only a segment, a country, a page type, with the same rules as decisions. Three, the groups. The first group is always the reference, here 'Business as Usual', the current Co-Buy logic. Then one or several variations: that's A/B/n. Here it's A/B, 50/50. We could add a third group, top sellers, and split 34/33/33, as long as it adds up to 100%. A visitor stays in the same group for the whole test, and the test takes priority over the zone's normal decisions. Four, when to stop: here at least 14 days and at most 90, at least 1,000 people per group, and a 95% confidence threshold. Once started, the test can't be edited, so the comparison stays fair. Results come in daily, the Bayesian way: for each group, the lift in %, a credible interval, the chance to beat the reference, and the chance to beat all groups. On our demo site there's no result yet, the traffic is too small."

UC: UC5.

Screen 4.2 — What's not there yet (honest moment)

Show: stay on the experiment.

Say: "Use case 5 asks for incrementality: what do recommendations add compared to no recommendations at all, in euros. Two honest limits today. One: every test group must show something. You can compare 'new logic versus current logic', but not 'recommendations versus nothing'. Two: results are a percentage lift on counts, add-to-cart, purchases, views. Not extra revenue in euros. For that you'd export the data to your BI. Both are on our list for the product team, and our product management team is here if you want to go deeper."
